1.Introduction
2025 marked the 20th anniversary since the start of operation of the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS). The year saw an increase in the number of extreme weather events with devastating health, social and economic effects. It also capped the hottest 11-year period (2015-2025) on record 1 , underscoring the need to reinforce climate mitigation measures, such as the EU ETS.
While consumption of coal continued to decrease in the EU as whole in 2025 and the supply of renewable energy remained stable 2 , the supply of gas increased for the second consecutive year (+ 2.3%), with 88% of gas supply in the grid coming from imports 3 .
The rapidly evolving economic and geopolitical context highlighted the benefit of the EU ETS in providing a clear, stable and long-term signal that guides EU economic operators away from fossil fuels, thus serving the EU’s decarbonisation, energy security and competitiveness goals.
Despite energy price variations due to international conflicts, the system continued to deliver emissions reductions based on a robust carbon price and liquidity of allowances. Indicators show a well-functioning and resilient market where predictability was further improved following key legislative proposals 4 tabled by the European Commission to adapt the system up to 2040.
As the oldest and most mature compliance carbon market at global level, the EU ETS also serves as an example for other countries interested in designing and developing carbon markets, supporting decarbonisation not only in Europe but also abroad.
This report focuses on the latest developments of the EU ETS in 2025 and the first months of 2026. It is adopted in line with Articles 10(5) and 21(2) of the ETS Directive (Directive 2003/87/EC) 5 . The information provided is based mostly on data from the Union Registry and national reporting.
Main findings
The report shows that overall emissions covered by the EU ETS continued the downward trend observed over the past years. Taken together, emissions from power, industry, aviation and maritime transport have decreased by 2% compared to 2024. Emissions from the power sector and industrial installations dropped respectively by 1.7% and 3.3% compared to 2024. Emissions from maritime transport decreased by 4% while emissions from aviation grew by 0,2%, confirming the pressing need to decarbonise the aviation sector.
The lower emissions in the maritime sector can be largely attributed to increased consumption of sustainable fuels, which are zero rated under the EU ETS. The increase in the aviation sector would also have been higher if not offset by the growing use of Sustainable Aviation Fuels (SAF), which accounted for approximately 1.7 million tonnes of CO₂ savings. Without this contribution, emissions in the sector would have increased by 1.3% in 2025.
Market oversight confirms the smooth running of auctioning and trading on the primary and secondary market of the EU ETS. The auctions of emission allowances and trading dynamics remained largely stable compared to previous years. The average EU ETS allowance price in 2025 was EUR 73.43, varying between EUR 59.76 and EUR 84.60 throughout the year.
Revenues from the EU ETS increased further in 2025, rising up to EUR 43.2 billion (+11% compared to 2024), confirming the system’s key role as a powerful financing engine. More than half of this amount (EUR 23.9 billion) went directly to EU Member States, allowing them to finance climate, energy and social measures to support households and companies in transitioning away from fossil fuels.
The remaining part of the revenues financed breakthrough innovation, solidarity, energy transition and energy security by providing €2.6 billion for the Innovation Fund and €6.5 billion for the Modernisation Fund. Auction revenues also supported the Recovery and Resilience Facility (RRF) to raise €20 billion for the REPowerEU Plan. The EUR 20 billion revenue target was reached in July 2026 after the auctioning of 278.5 million allowances.
For the first time in 2025, EUR 3.7 billion were raised also for the Social Climate Fund, to support vulnerable households and transport users during the transition to climate neutrality.
Finally, 2025 showed a very high compliance rate (99.5%) with the monitoring, reporting, verification and surrendering obligations of the EU ETS, confirming that over the past 20 years the EU system has become a robust and mature carbon market, stimulating investments in Europe’s transition and modernisation.
2.A broad and efficient emissions coverage
Scope and recent developments
The EU ETS, which started operating in 2005, has developed significantly over the last 20 years. It currently applies in the 27 EU Member States, as well as in Iceland, Norway and Lichtenstein. Although the United Kingdom is no longer part of the system, electricity generation plants in Northern Ireland remain covered by it.
Initially focused on emissions from the power sector and big industrial installations, the scope of the EU ETS has progressively expanded to cover emissions from the different transport sectors. Aviation and maritime emissions are covered under ETS1, while emissions from road transport, together with emissions from the combustion of fuels in buildings and smaller industry, fall under a separate system (ETS2).
In 2025, ETS1 thus applied to more than 12.500 entities established across the 30 countries in its geographical scope and Northern Ireland. The total emissions from stationary installations, aviation and maritime sectors on which the carbon price was applied amounted to 1 163 million tonnes of CO2e., representing about 37% of EU GHG emissions. With the addition of emissions covered under the new ETS2, the system will cover about 75% of EU GHG emissions.
2.1.1.Power and industrial installations
In 2025, the EU ETS covered more than 8 500 stationary installations. The majority of these (about 5 000 installations) were active in the heat and power sector, followed by industrial installations in the ceramics, paper, chemicals and glass sectors.
Emissions covered by the EU ETS include CO2 emissions but also other greenhouse gases:
·N2O (nitrous oxide) from nitric, adipic and glyoxylic acids and glyoxal production;
·PFCs (perfluorocarbons) from primary aluminium production.
In 2025, the total amount of emissions from stationary installations amounted to 1 013 Mt CO2eq, representing a 2% decrease compared to 2005. Section 3.1 provides further details on these emissions trends.
As of 2024, installations for incinerating municipal waste monitor and report their emissions under the EU ETS. However, they are not yet required to surrender allowances for their emissions. Emissions from waste treatment are currently subject to national reduction targets under the Effort Sharing Regulation (Regulation 2018/842).
2.1.2.Aviation
Aviation transport accounts for around 3-4% of the EU’s CO2 emissions.
The EU ETS has regulated emissions from the aviation sector since 2012. Legally, under the EU ETS Directive, the system covers all outgoing flights from to the European Economic Area (EEA), and all incoming flights, unless exempted. The EU, however, decided to temporarily limit the scope of the EU ETS to flights within the EEA, to support the development of a global measure by the International Civil Aviation Organisation (ICAO).
CO2 emissions from aircraft operators continued to slightly increase in 2025 compared to the preceding year. In 2025, fossil-fuel related CO2 emissions from aviation amounted to 62.7 Mt (including 988 434 tons of operators administered by Switzerland), up from 62.6 Mt in 2024. This is an increase of around 0.2% since 2024, underlining the urgent need to decarbonise the aviation sector. The ReFuelEU Aviation Regulation sets a framework to increase the production and uptake of sustainable aviation fuels (SAF) in the EU. 1.7 million tonnes of CO2 were saved due to the use of SAF, which is treated as zero-emission under the EU ETS.
In 2025, around 25.4 million allowances were issued in respect of aircraft operators in line with the limited scope of the EU ETS. To advance emission reductions in the sector, in 2025 50% fewer free allowances were allocated to aircraft operators than in 2023. The resulting number after the reductions amounted to 10.9 million allowances. Aircraft operators administered by national administrators in the EEA received a little more than 0.2 million Swiss allowances for free under the Swiss ETS. Aircraft operators received about 2.9 million allowances for the use of SAF. Approximately 11.6 million allowances were auctioned in 2025 in respect of aircraft operators.
The ETS carbon price already provides an incentive of around EUR 250 per tonne of SAF used, compared to the price of fossil kerosene through zero-rating. In 2025, the Commission started implementing an additional support mechanism under the EU ETS for the use of eligible sustainable aviation fuels uplifted on ETS routes. For each tonne of eligible fuel used on an ETS route, airlines can claim support from EUR500 up to EUR7000 6 . For 2025, the EU ETS incentive by zero rating SAF was worth around EUR 135 million. This was supplemented by dedicated ETS support of about 5.2 million allowances worth approximately EUR 430 million 7 .
In a significant step to improve transparency, the Commission is publishing more data on aviation emissions, while protecting commercially sensitive data.
The analysis below is provided under Article 30(8) of the ETS Directive. This report includes, for the first time, additional information on short flights, private and business jets, the social impacts of the EU ETS Directive on the aviation sector and the connectivity of outermost regions (OMRs). More details can be found in the supporting study, impact assessment, and Annex 10 to the impact assessment on the revised ETS Directive proposed in July 2026.
Regarding short flights, the EU ETS covers 96% of short-haul flights emissions. In 2019, flights under 1000 km produced 18% of CO₂ from departing EU/EEA flights, and short-haul traffic is expected to grow by 25% by 2040 compared with 2025 8 . Replacing short-haul flights with high-speed rail and ferries has the biggest potential to cut emissions on short-haul routes. Sustainable aviation fuel can also help, while electric, hybrid and hydrogen aircraft remain promising longer-term options.
Private and business jets, although accounting only for a small share of aviation emissions, emit 5 to 14 times more CO₂ per passenger than commercial flights and are backed by far greater financial resources. Under Eurocontrol’s high-growth scenario, business aviation is projected to grow by 1.4% annually from 2024 to 2050 9 . The Commission therefore proposes to include private and business jets in the EU ETS (67% of private-jet emissions are currently exempt), while simplifying the rules for operators and exempting those with very low surrender obligations. As business aviation is used by only a small number of people, its impact on overall connectivity is limited.
Regarding social impacts of the EU ETS Directive on the aviation sector, modelling indicates that, by 2040, employment among EEA‑based aircraft operators will have increased by 26%-28% compared with 2024. Additional SAF support could stimulate further investment and European SAF plants are projected to create 0.2–1.8 jobs per 100 tonnes of SAF produced annually. According to the model, although ticket prices rise across all segments, with the strongest relative effects seen on low-cost carrier fares, passenger demand continues to grow across all route types by more than 30% in 2040 compared to 2024. This indicates that underlying demand growth outweighs the dampening effect of higher fares. 
Regarding the connectivity of outermost regions (OMR), islands and remote territories, almost all of them recorded more flights in 2024 than in 2019. In the OMR, emissions from non‑domestic EEA flights covered by the EU ETS rose by 14% between 2019 and 2024, exceeding 6% of total aviation‑ETS emissions. The Commission proposes to no longer exempt those routes as of 2031. The EU ETS already includes special provisions to safeguard their air connectivity. Member States may designate socially necessary flights as public service obligations (PSOs) to preserve connectivity on routes that would otherwise be underserved. 2.1.3.Maritime transport
Maritime transport is a major emitter of greenhouse gases accounting for around 3% to 4% of the EU’s CO2 emissions. As of 1 January 2024, emissions from maritime transport activities are included in the EU ETS as part of the EU’s overall strategy to decarbonise shipping. The system covers 50% of emissions from voyages starting or ending outside of the EEA and 100% of emissions that occur between two EEA ports and when ships are within an EEA port.
This extension of the EU ETS builds on the provisions in place for other sectors covered by the EU ETS system as well as the Maritime Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (MRV) Regulation. For 2024 and 2025, the EU ETS for maritime transport only covered CO2 emissions, and will be extended to CH4 and N2O from 2026. For 2025, shipping companies reported, 87.4 MtCO₂ under the EU ETS scope which was around 4% lower than what reported for 2024, a decrease mainly due to the uptake of zero-rated sustainable fuels.
Shipping companies’ obligation to surrender allowances is being introduced gradually, through a phase-in period lasting until 2026. During this period, shipping companies are required to surrender allowances corresponding to 40% of verified emissions reported for 2024, 70% of verified emissions reported for 2025 and, for emissions generated in 2026 onwards, 100% of the verified emissions covered by the EU ETS. A further reduction is made for ice-class vessels.
The surrendering requirements, after application of deductions and the phase-in coefficient for 2025 (of 70%), corresponded to 60.8 million EU allowances. To bridge the gap between verified emissions and surrendered allowances from maritime transport, 26 720 603 allowances are being cancelled for 2025.
The ETS Directive includes a reporting and review clause to monitor the implementation of the EU ETS for maritime transport, in particular to detect and address any evasive behaviour at an early stage. In addition, with the ETS review proposal, the Commission aims to make the system more effective by reducing the risk of evasive practices, extending certain derogations and ensuring a level playing field through the extension of the ETS scope to certain categories of smaller vessels (between 400 and 5 000 gross tonnage). 2.1.4.Buildings, road transport and additional sectors
As part of the 2023 revision of the ETS Directive, a new emissions trading system (ETS2) was created to cover CO2 emissions from fuel combustion in buildings, road transport and additional sectors (mainly lower emissions industry sectors not covered by the existing EU ETS). ETS2 complements national emission reduction targets under the Effort Sharing Regulation.
ETS2 is separate from the existing EU ETS. Although both are ‘cap and trade’ systems, ETS2 covers emissions upstream. This means that fuel suppliers (regulated entities) are required to monitor and report their emissions corresponding to the combustion of fuels that they release for consumption in the sectors falling under the ETS2 scope. Once the system is fully operational, the regulated entities will be required to buy and surrender allowances covering their emissions of the previous year.
With the amendment to the European Climate Law adopted in spring 2026 10 , the surrendering obligation under ETS2 was postponed by one year from 2027 to 2028. The amendment also triggered the required changes of a one-year postponement, set out in Article 30k(2) of the ETS Directive, of dates and deadlines, notably as regards auctioning, surrendering and review 11 . By June 2027, the EU-wide quantity of allowances, i.e. the ETS2 cap, will be established for the year 2028 based on the average CO2 emissions reported by the ETS2 regulated entities for the years 2024 to 2026. The first surrendering will take place by 31 May 2029.
In autumn 2025, the Commission announced a package of measures to ensure an even more gradual and smooth start of ETS2 and worked towards its implementation. In June 2026, the changes to the Auctioning Regulation entered into force, allowing for an early start of ETS2 auctions to make revenues available already in 2027, enabling early investments and delivering an early price signal 12 . In October 2026, the targeted adjustments to the Market Stability Reserve Decision regarding ETS2 were adopted by the European Parliament and the Council 13 . The EU ETS in the context of international carbon pricing
EU-Swiss ETS Linking Agreement
The EU and Swiss Emissions Trading Systems have been linked since 2020, on the basis of a Linking Agreement 15 that ensures mutual recognition of allowances issued by the two parties. This provides EU and Swiss operators with access to the two markets, allowing them to purchase and surrender allowances in either of the two systems.
The Linking Agreement ensures that the two parties apply the same approach to rules on geographical coverage, cap and allocation of allowances. It applies to the full scope of ETS1, i.e. to emissions from stationary, maritime and aviation sectors. Swiss domestic flights and flights departing to EEA airports are covered by the Swiss ETS, while flights departing from the EEA to Switzerland fall under the EU ETS.
Since 2024, transfers of allowances between the two systems have been performed daily, using a direct link between their respective registries. In 2025, the total number of transfers from the EU ETS to the Swiss ETS reached 3 692 716 (+ 132% compared to 2024). The total number of transfers from the Swiss ETS to the EU ETS reached 3 606 093 (+129% compared to 2024), showing sustained and increasing interest from market participants (see Table 20 of accompanying technical staff working document).
In total, EU stationary installations and aircraft operators used 930 188 allowances issued under the Swiss ETS for compliance under the EU ETS in 2025 (0.1% of all surrendered allowances for compliance under the EU ETS excluding maritime surrendered allowances). Conversely, Swiss stationary installations and aircraft operators used 855 225 allowances issued under the Swiss ETS, representing 16% of all allowances surrendered under the Swiss ETS (see Table 19 of accompanying technical staff working document).
After five years of effective operation, the link between the EU and Swiss emissions trading systems has proven to be an efficient tool for ensuring convergence between the two carbon markets, increasing liquidity, offering flexibility and aligning carbon prices across jurisdictions.
EU-UK ETS Linking Agreement
In May 2025, the first EU-UK Summit since the UK’s withdrawal from the EU took place. At this summit, leaders agreed on a renewed agenda for European Union – United Kingdom cooperation. Among other agreements that are foreseen, the Common Understanding includes the ambition to work towards linking the EU ETS and the UK ETS. A linked marked would address many of the issues raised in respect of trade and a level playing field. Since the adoption of the negotiation mandate by the Council in November 2025, the European Commission has been negotiating a linking agreement with the relevant UK authorities. As of 1 October 2026, these negotiations have not yet been concluded.
EU engagement on global carbon markets
The European Commission established a Task Force on International Carbon Pricing and Markets Diplomacy as part of its Communication on the 2040 Climate Target. Building on the EU’s successful experience with the EU ETS, as well as on its longstanding international engagement, the Task Force shares the EU’s lessons learned and encourages more partner countries to introduce carbon pricing tools tailored to their needs. It also engages actively in international carbon markets to strengthen climate ambition and contribute to sustainable development. The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) has further incentivised governments to use pricing measures to reduce GHG emissions.
The EU’s outreach and diplomacy have contributed to tangible progress. Encouraged by international collaboration, carbon pricing and markets have continued to gain traction as more countries integrate them into their strategies to meet Paris Agreement commitments. In 2025, 28% of global GHG emissions were subject to a carbon price 16 , with around 80 jurisdictions having a carbon pricing policy in place or under development. Some of the most prominent developments over the past months include the adoption of legislation setting up or extending carbon pricing schemes in Brazil, China, India, Japan and Türkiye.
Annual Carbon Market Workshop under the Florence Process
The Carbon Market Workshop, held under the Florence Process, is the European Commission’s international forum on carbon pricing. Established in 2017 and jointly organised with the European University Institute (EUI), it takes place annually as part of the EUI Climate Week. The Climate Week combines public sessions on climate policy developments, including carbon pricing, followed by the closed-door Carbon Market Workshop for participating jurisdictions and a limited number of invited experts.
The workshop brings together both established and emerging emissions trading jurisdictions to exchange experience and discuss solutions to common challenges in policy implementation. Participants include the EU, Switzerland, the UK, Brazil, New Zealand, China, the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI), California, Canada and Québec. With an expansion of carbon pricing globally, more jurisdictions were invited to the workshop in recent years, including Türkiye and Mexico. The Florence Process exchange is highly relevant in the context of the EU’s 2040 climate target, outreach on carbon pricing and engagement on international carbon markets under the Paris Agreement (Article 6).
In 2026, the Florence Process took place on 7 May. Discussions focused on recent developments in participating jurisdictions, the role of carbon pricing in driving investment in industrial decarbonisation, and the integration of carbon removals and international credits into emissions trading system design.
Open Coalition on Compliance Markets
On 7 May 2026, the EU, Brazil and China launched the Open Coalition on Compliance Markets (OCCCM). The Coalition was joined by New Zealand and Germany on the same day, and by the United Kingdom, Norway and Canada subsequently. As of July 2026, several other countries and organisations have submitted, or are in the process of submitting, an expression of interest to join the Coalition, and are expected to join in the course of the year. The establishment of the OCCCM follows on a Leaders Declaration on Carbon Markets signed by 18 countries at COP30.
The purpose of the Coalition is to advance international cooperation on compliance carbon markets as an important policy tool for achieving Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement. In concrete terms, the Coalition will strengthen collaboration and exchange of best practices in areas such as monitoring, reporting and verification systems, carbon accounting methodologies, and potential use of high integrity offsets to promote environmental integrity.
