[제목] Remarks by Commissioner Dombrovskis on simplification at the Employers Group of the European Economic and Social Committee

Good afternoon.
Thank you for the invitation to join you today to present our simplification agenda, what we hope it can achieve and why it is so necessary at this pivotal moment.
Let me begin by setting out the broader context.
As you are all aware, we are navigating a changing and challenging global landscape.
Continuing with business as usual is no longer an option.
It is clear that Europe must urgently take action to secure our long-term prosperity and security.
That imperative lies at the heart of the Commission's competitiveness agenda.
In short, Europe must become more dynamic, innovative and productive to compete effectively in the world, defend its values and maintain our standard of living.
Following on from last year's Draghi Report, the Commission issued its Competitiveness Compass in January.
It sets out our strategic roadmap aimed at restoring Europe's dynamism and enhancing economic growth.
Simplification – or, to put it simply, cutting red tape – is a central element of building a competitive Europe.
In fact, you could say that it is the key to unlocking Europe's full competitive potential.
As you all know, the past five years were a period of intense regulatory activity.
This generated a large and still expanding regulatory burden.
As we take stock, we see that this accumulation of rules, and their increased complexity, have become problems in themselves.
We have been overwhelming European businesses with a huge volume of regulatory demands.
Today, regulation is seen by more than 60% of EU companies as an obstacle to investment.
55% of SMEs flag regulatory obstacles and administrative burdens as their greatest challenge.
Of course, SMEs are Europe's economic engine, accounting for 99% of all businesses in the EU.
They also have limited resources to devote to paperwork.
The sheer volume of regulatory demands is stifling innovation, deterring investment, and putting jobs at risk.
This in turn makes our citizens less prosperous and undermines our ability to achieve our economic, social and environmental goals.
So, at its core, simplification is about addressing this problem by making sure our rules and regulations simpler, more proportionate and easier to navigate.
This will allow our businesses to redirect the time, energy, and resources that businesses currently devote to paperwork towards more productive and beneficial pursuits.
And that will result in more jobs, innovation and economic growth that will facilitate the achievement of our economic, social and security objectives.
While I have been tasked with coordinating work the Commission's work on simplification, each Commissioner will be responsible for stress-testing the EU acquis under their respective portfolios.
We aim to review the whole acquis by the end of the current mandate.
While the challenge is great, the level of our ambition is equal to it.
EUROSTAT has estimated the baseline of overall recurring administrative costs for companies and administrations in the EU at €150 billion annually.
We have set out ambitious targets for all administrative costs: reducing them by 25% for all companies and 35% for SMEs.
This translates into cutting approximately €37.5 billion in annual administrative costs by the end of this Commission's mandate.
So, how do we achieve this?
We have a clear roadmap to guide our work.
Earlier this year, the Commission adopted our overall strategy on implementation and simplification for the next five years, entitled “A simpler and faster Europe”.
It is constructed around four building blocks:
ensuring EU policies deliver results,
making Europe simpler and faster,
improving the way we make new rules,
and strengthening our work with co-legislators and stakeholders.
Taken together, these four building blocks signal a change in the regulatory culture of the European Commission.
They shift the focus towards ensuring that EU rules are as simple and cost-effective as possible, and that they deliver on the ground.
On the fourth block, strengthening our work with co-legislators and stakeholders, I would like to stress that simplification is a joint effort that requires close cooperation with Member States, and EU institutions and bodies.
In this respect, we hope to count on the expertise and support of the EESC so that we can deliver changes that make a real difference.
And I am pleased to report today that our work on implementing the simplification agenda is already well underway.
The Commission's Work Programme for 2025 outlines a long list of proposals that will focus on simplification.
We are moving through this programme quickly and with purpose.
In February, we introduced our first two proposals to simplify EU rules in the fields of sustainability reporting, sustainability due-diligence, taxonomy, CBAM and public investments.
Earlier this month, we presented proposals to support Europe's farmers with the simplification of CAP, so making rules that are simpler, more proportionate, and better tailored to their needs.
We also introduced a new category of small-mid cap to make compliance obligations more proportionate and cut costs for EU businesses.
Taken together, a conservative estimate puts the annual savings for businesses stemming from these proposals at over €8 billion.
And the good news is that we are just getting started.
In the coming weeks and months, we will come forward with further proposals for the defence, digital and chemical sectors.
Of course, the input and feedback we receive from those businesses navigating our rules and regulations play a key role in designing our simplification proposals.
This has involved intensive engagement with representatives from businesses – large and small – in industry, and the corporate world.
We have been listening – seeking their views on what real changes can make a real difference on the ground for people and businesses.
Specifically, we have introduced the two new consultation tools, namely reality checks and implementation dialogues.
Using reality checks, the Commission is reaching out directly to practitioners to identify practical issues they face.
The reality checks aim to provide concrete insights that the Commission could use to further develop solutions to reduce regulatory costs or to improve the workability of obligations.
At the political level, each Commissioner will carry out two implementation dialogues per year to better identify inefficiencies when applying EU laws.
These dialogues will seek feedback from stakeholders on best practices, poor implementation, gold plating, over-compliance or fragmentation, and uncover opportunities for simplification and implementation support.
Four Implementation Dialogues have already taken place in Brussels on topics from permitting to defence, with many more in the pipeline before the summer recess.
The outcome of the reality checks and implementation dialogues will inform the stress-testing of the EU acquis, which could result in evaluations or fitness checks or future simplification proposals.
So, you see, we are taking engagement with stakeholders very seriously.
Today, represents another important opportunity for such engagement.
The EESC's Employers' Group brings together over 100 entrepreneurs and representatives of business associations from every EU Member State.
You represent more than 20 million European companies – large and small – across every sector.
You are very well place to act as the voice of European businesses, articulating their day-to-day realities, challenges and opportunities.
This openness means that we very much welcome your input on how we can improve the quality of EU rules and reduce regulatory and compliance burdens.
Simplification is far from a “simple” endeavour.
It is far easier to make complex rules than to make simple ones.
Yet, simple rules are usually more effective, while complex frameworks tend to underachieve their objectives and generate higher costs.
We have found in your study a lot of points in common with the Commission's own strategy on implementation and simplification.
Both recognise the need to reduce regulatory burdens and monitor the progress of that reduction.
They both aim at enhancing implementation and ensuring that new regulations are clear, predictable and proportionate.
Our approach also places businesses at the centre of this process through the new consultation tools – implementation dialogues and reality checks – that I referred to earlier.
The Commission remains committed to realising our economic, social and environmental goals.
But is precisely to achieve those goals that we must hear directly from those who bear the responsibility of applying EU rules.
Your study makes several interesting proposals.
It suggests, for instance, introducing a regulatory burden index.
We are following a similar approach by defining targets for administrative costs savings and, by applying the Standard Cost Model.
This enables us to assess and monitor the contribution of EU proposals to the overall burden reduction target.
The study also highlights the potential of using AI and other innovative IT tools to identify the opportunities for simplification and improve the quality of new legislation.
And actually, the Commission has begun using AI to analyse input received from public consultations and to foster the consistency of regulatory provisions across legal acts.
We are also working to integrate AI technologies to, for example, detect reporting obligations or flag potential gold-plating.
This effort is complex and requires time to be developed, but it is one of our priorities. And, of course, human involvement remains essential.
The inputs from your study are, therefore, timely and appreciated.
I will stop here by thanking you, once again, for your invitation today.
I look forward to hearing your experiences, concerns and suggestions in our exchange following my remarks.
Thank you.
