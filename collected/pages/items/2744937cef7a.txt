Extension of CBAM to Iceland and Norway
Extension of CBAM to Iceland and Norway
News article
18 November 2026
Directorate-General for Taxation and Customs Union
3 min read
The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) will be extended to Iceland and Norway following the entry into force of the European Economic Area (EEA) Joint Committee Decision No 283/2026 on 18 November 2026. As of 1 January 2027, CBAM will apply in Norway and Iceland to goods imported into their customs territories, in the same way that it applies to goods entering the EU customs territory.
The extension follows a decision by the EEA Joint Committee, incorporating Regulation (EU) 2023/956 establishing the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), into the EEA Agreement. Lichtenstein remains outside of the scope of the CBAM area.
The extension ensures a more consistent application of carbon pricing rules across a single CBAM area and reflects the shared commitment towards ambitious decarbonisation, fair competition and greater transparency on the embedded emissions of imported goods.
What does this mean in practice?
Annual threshold to apply cumulatively across the single CBAM area
CBAM applies to all imports of electricity and hydrogen, regardless of the quantity, and to imports above an annual cumulative threshold of 50 tonnes in four sectors: aluminium, iron and steel, fertilisers, and cement. To ensure the uniform application of CBAM across the single CBAM area, this threshold will apply cumulatively to imports into the customs territories of the EU, Norway and Iceland taken together.
First steps for importers established in Norway or Iceland
CBAM started its definitive period on 1 January 2026 in the EU. From 1 January 2027, CBAM will apply to imports of CBAM goods into Norway and Iceland. Importers of CBAM goods established in Norway or Iceland that exceed the applicable threshold, or their indirect customs representatives, will need to become authorized CBAM declarants, with certain flexibilities available to those who submit their authorisation applications by 31 March 2027.
Applications for the status of authorised CBAM declarant must be submitted through the Authorisation Management Module (AMM) of the CBAM Registry prior to the import of CBAM goods. The AMM enables importers and indirect customs representatives to apply in their EU country of establishment. Relevant information and links to the AMM are available here.
Mutual recognition of CBAM authorisations
Mutual recognition of CBAM authorisations will apply across the CBAM area. In practice, Norway and Iceland will not be considered third countries for the purposes of CBAM. Hence, an importer established in Norway or Iceland will be able to use a CBAM authorisation issued in these countries to import into the EU, and vice versa. Importers established in Norway or Iceland will not need to rely on an indirect customs representative for CBAM purposes when importing CBAM goods into the EU, and vice versa.
Identification numbers of operators: EORI and TIN
CBAM is designed to rely on the Economic Operator Registration and Identification (EORI) system, which customs authorities in the EU use to verify the existence and validity of CBAM account numbers. Norway and Iceland use a Trader Identification Number (TIN), and not EORI numbers.
For imports into Norway and Iceland, customs authorities will therefore verify the CBAM account number issued in the EFTA States against the corresponding TIN. For imports into the EU, importers established in Norway or Iceland will need to obtain an EORI number before importing goods into the customs territory of the Union. Similarly, EU-established companies wishing to import into Norway or Iceland on the basis of an EU CBAM account will need to obtain a TIN.
Registration of accredited verifiers
The registration of verifiers accredited by Norway and Iceland will begin on 1 December 2026.
Background
CBAM is a key part of the EU's climate policy framework. It addresses the risk of carbon leakage and encourages cleaner industrial production by ensuring that the price of certain imports reflects their embedded carbon emissions.
